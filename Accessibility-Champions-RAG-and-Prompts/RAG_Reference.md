# Accessibility Champions Network Guidance

## Purpose

This guidance provides a practical, organization-ready framework for establishing, resourcing, and managing an Accessibility Champions Network. It is designed for medium to large organizations that want to embed accessibility into everyday operations, improve compliance maturity, and build a more inclusive workplace and customer experience.

The network is not a stand-alone awareness campaign. It is an operational capability that distributes accessibility ownership across teams, supports leaders, and helps turn accessibility from a specialist activity into a shared organizational responsibility.

---

## 1. What an Accessibility Champions Network is

An Accessibility Champions Network is a cross-functional community of people embedded within teams who promote accessibility in their day-to-day work. Champions act as connectors between policy, delivery, and lived experience. They help teams understand accessibility requirements, identify issues early, support colleagues, and encourage a culture of continuous learning.

The model works best when it includes:

- a senior executive sponsor
- a dedicated network manager or coordinator
- business-level champions across major divisions
- team-based accessibility champions
- clear responsibilities, time allocation, and reporting lines
- a structured training and engagement model
- measurable outcomes and regular reporting

---

## 2. Why organizations should establish one

A champions network improves accessibility maturity by moving responsibility beyond a central specialist team. It creates local ownership, supports early intervention, and improves organizational learning.

It helps organizations:

- reduce legal and compliance risk
- improve product and service quality
- strengthen inclusion and employee experience
- increase awareness and confidence across teams
- reduce rework and post-launch remediation costs
- build a sustainable accessibility culture

---

## 3. Recommended governance model

### 3.1 Executive sponsor

The executive sponsor is the senior leader accountable for the program and for ensuring it has authority, visibility, and budget.

Typical sponsor roles:

- Chief Digital Officer
- Chief Compliance Officer
- Chief People Officer
- COO or divisional leader

Responsibilities:

- secure and protect funding
- align accessibility goals to business priorities
- remove organizational blockers
- sponsor accessibility as a strategic capability, not a checkbox activity
- review network outcome metrics and hold leaders accountable

The sponsor should be visible and active. Without senior sponsorship, the network risks becoming unfunded, low-priority, or disconnected from operational decision-making.

### 3.2 Champions Network Manager

The network manager ensures the program is organized, active, and sustainable. This role should be treated as a real operational function, not an informal volunteer assignment.

Responsibilities:

- recruit and onboard champions
- coordinate training and development
- maintain community channels and working groups
- monitor participation and track outcomes
- report on progress and barriers to leadership
- maintain toolkits, templates, and best practices
- create a recognition program to keep engagement high

Recommended resourcing:

- small organization: 0.5 FTE
- medium organization: 0.75 to 1.0 FTE
- large enterprise: 1 to 3 FTE depending on complexity and geographic spread

### 3.3 Business accessibility champions

Business accessibility champions support large groups or divisions and act as a bridge between local teams and the central program.

They typically:

- coordinate champion activity within a business unit
- translate strategic objectives into operational actions
- identify cross-team barriers and escalate issues
- support measurement and reporting for their area
- help maintain momentum during change and restructuring

### 3.4 Team accessibility champions

Team champions are embedded within departments, service teams, or delivery groups. They are not expected to do all accessibility work themselves; they are advocates, coordinators, and local enablers.

Typical responsibilities:

- include accessibility in sprint planning and team routines
- review work against accessibility standards and requirements
- answer basic accessibility questions
- share learning and examples with the team
- escalate issues needing specialist input or leadership support
- support onboarding and local practice improvements

A practical model is one champion per team or department, or roughly one champion for every 5 to 10 employees in larger organizations.

---

## 4. Operating model and responsibilities

A successful network follows a clear operating structure:

```text
Executive Sponsor
    |
    v
Champions Network Manager
    |
    +--> Business Accessibility Champions
    |       |
    |       +--> Team Accessibility Champions
    |
    +--> Community channels and learning activities
    |
    +--> Reporting, governance, and recognition
```

### Core design principles

1. Champions are embedded, not isolated
   - They work in real teams and with real work.
   - They support colleagues rather than acting as the sole accessibility gatekeepers.

2. Accessibility is shared accountability
   - The network amplifies organizational capability.
   - It does not replace the responsibility of leaders, product owners, designers, engineers, or managers.

3. Learning is continuous
   - Champions need ongoing development, not one-off training.
   - Accessibility maturity grows through practice, reflection, and shared learning.

4. Recognition sustains effort
   - Champions need visible support and appreciation.
   - Contribution should be recognized in performance, communication, and leadership forums.

5. Measurement drives progress
   - The network should track both participation and outcomes.
   - Reporting should show business value, not just activity counts.

---

## 5. How to establish the network

### Phase 1: Foundation (Months 1-3)

Objectives:

- confirm sponsorship and governance
- define role expectations and responsibilities
- recruit the first group of champions
- establish the community and communications model
- deliver initial training

Actions:

- appoint an executive sponsor
- assign or hire the network manager
- define the network charter and champion role description
- identify pilot teams and likely champions
- set up a community platform such as Microsoft Teams or Slack
- agree on training requirements and reporting cadence
- establish a baseline assessment of current accessibility maturity
- join CAN (Champions of Accessibility Network)

Success indicators:

- sponsor in place
- manager assigned
- initial champion group established
- active community channel established
- foundational training completed

### Phase 2: Scale (Months 4-12)

Objectives:

- expand coverage across teams
- embed accessibility into planning and delivery routines
- build tiered training pathways
- establish recurring knowledge-sharing activities

Actions:

- recruit champions across major departments
- integrate accessibility reviews into sprint planning and design reviews
- add role-specific learning for product, engineering, design, QA, and management
- run monthly or quarterly learning sessions
- create a shared resource library and examples of good practice
- report progress to leadership on a regular schedule

Success indicators:

- most teams have a named champion
- champion coverage reaches planned target levels
- accessibility issues are tracked and escalated consistently
- training completion is above target
- visible improvements in product and service quality are being reported

### Phase 3: Maturity (Months 12-24)

Objectives:

- institutionalize the network into organizational practice
- improve sustainability and resilience
- link accessibility to leadership and performance systems

Actions:

- integrate accessibility into procurement, project governance, and onboarding
- strengthen advanced training and specialist expertise
- monitor network health and champion retention
- connect accessibility metrics to business performance and risk reporting
- review governance, scope, and recognition annually

Success indicators:

- high network coverage and participation
- accessibility embedded in operational decision-making
- champions are active across business units
- regular reporting demonstrates measurable impact

## 6. Resourcing model

A successful champion network requires resources beyond goodwill. It should be funded and planned as a business capability.

### Recommended annual investment profile

For a mid-sized organization of around 500 people, a realistic starting model may include:

- Champions Network Manager: 0.5 to 1.0 FTE
- Training, tools, and learning platform costs: $20,000 to $30,000+
- Community tools and communication platform support: $2,000 to $10,000+
- Recognition and events: $5,000 to $15,000+
- Optional CAN corporate sponsorship: $10,000
- Champion time allocation: embedded within existing roles and budgets

This is not just a training budget. It is an investment in organizational capability, risk reduction, and customer value.

### Time allocation guidance

Champions should not be expected to carry accessibility work without protected time. A practical model is:

- team champions: 5 to 10 hours per week, adjusted by team size and maturity
- business champions: additional coordination time and reporting responsibility
- network manager: dedicated operational ownership and community maintenance

If the role is added on top of regular work without explicit time allocation, engagement tends to drop and the network becomes unsustainable.

---

## 7. Recruitment and selection of champions

The most effective champions are not necessarily the most technical people. They are often the people who are trusted, proactive, collaborative, and able to influence others.

Look for people who:

- are respected by their peers
- have strong communication and problem-solving skills
- are curious and willing to learn
- can work across functions and teams
- can influence without formal authority
- are motivated by inclusion and product quality

Selection approach:

- ask teams to nominate champions
- ensure representation across product, engineering, design, operations, support, and business functions
- avoid relying on a single discipline or department only
- provide role expectations in writing
- confirm manager support before appointment

---

## 8. Training and development framework

Training should be structured, role-based, and ongoing.

### Foundational training

For all new champions.

Topics:

- accessibility fundamentals
- WCAG and legal context
- common barriers and user needs
- assistive technologies and inclusive design
- how accessibility fits into everyday work
- responsibilities of champions and the network

Duration: 8 to 16 hours

### Intermediate training

For champions with more responsibility and scope.

Topics:

- testing methods and remediation approaches
- accessible design and development practices
- stakeholder coaching and influence
- measurement, reporting, and escalation
- supporting team change and adoption

Duration: 12 to 20 hours

### Advanced training

For network leaders and business champions.

Topics:

- accessibility strategy and governance
- integrating accessibility into organizational planning
- organizational maturity and ROI
- internal communications and culture change
- vendor, procurement, and cross-team coordination

This level should be reinforced through coaching, peer support, and learning from real delivery examples.

### Role-specific learning paths

Support champions according to their role:

- product managers: accessibility requirements, user research, prioritization
- designers: inclusive design patterns, contrast, keyboard flows, form design
- developers: semantic HTML, keyboard behavior, testing frameworks
- QA: testing strategies, assistive technology review, defect prioritization
- managers: embedding accessibility in planning, performance, and governance

---

## 9. Community, culture, and engagement

Culture is a major determinant of sustainability. A champion network will fail if it is treated like a side task or a compliance add-on.

### Recommended engagement model

- weekly or bi-weekly champion huddles
- monthly deep-dive learning sessions
- quarterly community forum or showcase
- dedicated chat or team channel for questions and fast support
- shared documents and examples of accessible practice

### Recognition approaches

Organizations should actively recognize champions so participation remains rewarding and visible.

Examples:

- monthly spotlights on team wins
- digital certificates or badges
- internal showcases and leadership updates
- professional development support
- inclusion in performance planning and promotion considerations

Recognition is particularly important where champions are doing extra work without formal authority.

---

## 10. Metrics that matter

The network needs a simple but credible measurement system. Track both network health and organizational impact.

### Network health metrics

- number of champions and coverage by team
- participation rate in meetings and channels
- training completion rates
- retention of champions over time
- satisfaction and perceived support
- time taken to resolve accessibility issues

### Organizational impact metrics

- reduction in accessibility defects before launch
- reduction in accessibility incidents after launch
- number of teams embedding accessibility into sprints or planning
- progress against WCAG and relevant standards
- improvements in user satisfaction and access outcomes
- employee feedback on inclusion and support

### Reporting cadence

- weekly: operational updates to the network manager and sponsor
- monthly: metrics review with leadership and business unit leads
- quarterly: business impact review and strategy alignment
- annually: maturity review and next-year plan

Leadership should receive a narrative alongside the numbers. Good reporting connects activity to outcomes, barriers, and decisions needed.

---

## 11. Common risks and how to mitigate them

| Risk | Likely consequence | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No executive sponsorship | Weak authority, low funding, poor sustainability | Secure sponsor early and provide visible support |
| Champions treated as volunteers only | Burnout, inconsistent engagement | Allocate time, recognition, and manager support |
| No dedicated program owner | Poor coordination and weak community | Assign a network manager with clear mandate |
| Training is one-off | Knowledge fades and behavior is inconsistent | Use ongoing, role-based learning |
| Accessibility is not embedded in workflows | Network becomes symbolic rather than operational | Link champions to sprint planning, design reviews, and QA |
| No measurable outcomes | Lack of leadership confidence and budget confidence | Track metrics from the outset |
| Network only exists in technical teams | Limited enterprise-wide impact | Include business, HR, operations, and service teams |

---

## 12. Governance and accountability

A champions network should be governed with clarity and consistency.

The network manager should maintain:

- a charter or operating model
- champion role definitions and selection criteria
- a training schedule
- reporting templates and dashboards
- meeting agendas and actions
- escalation process for blockers or unresolved issues

The sponsor should review

- progress against plan
- resource needs
- blocker resolution
- strategic decisions that affect network maturity

This helps keep the network active, credible, and aligned with business goals.

---

## 14. Key success factors

The strongest networks share a few common characteristics:

- senior sponsorship and visible leadership support
- adequate funding and time allocation
- dedicated coordination and governance
- strong inclusive culture and psychologically safe learning environment
- ongoing training instead of one-off activity
- measurable outcomes and regular reporting
- recognition and respect for champions
- cross-functional participation beyond technical teams

---

## 15. Conclusion

An Accessibility Champions Network is not a side initiative. It is a strategic capability for embedding accessibility into how an organization works, learns, and makes decisions. When properly established, resourced, and managed, it helps teams become more accessible, reduces organizational risk, and creates a culture where accessibility is everyone’s responsibility.

The most effective networks are built with intention. They start with executive sponsorship, dedicate operational support, assign credible local champions, provide structured learning, and track outcomes that matter to the business. With those foundations in place, accessibility can move from a specialist function to a durable and valued part of the organization.

---

## Suggested next steps

If your organization wants to launch this model, the immediate next steps are:

1. confirm executive support and sponsor
2. define network scope and champions structure
3. allocate a dedicated network manager role
4. recruit the first cohort of champions
5. establish governance, training, and reporting routines
6. review progress after 90 days and refine the model

This creates a practical foundation for building a sustainable Accessibility Champions Network that is meaningful, measurable, and operationally embedded.

- Assumption: context that is reasonable but not explicitly confirmed;
- Inference: a conclusion drawn from repository content or user context;
- Recommendation: a suggested action based on the above;
- Unknown: missing information that affects confidence or completeness.

Agents SHOULD use phrases such as:
- "Based on this repository...";
- "Assumption: ...";
- "Inference: ...";
- "Unknown: ...";
- "This recommendation requires human confirmation...";
- "This is a repository-based recommendation, not an organizational decision."

Agents MUST avoid unsupported claims such as:
- "This is required by policy" when no policy is provided;
- "This has been validated" without evidence;
- "This will achieve X outcome" without stated assumptions and limitations;
- "This is mandatory in all organisations" when the repository presents guidance rather than universally binding rules.

## Output expectations

When creating outputs from this repository, agents SHOULD use bounded formats such as:
- executive summary;
- phased roadmap;
- roles and responsibilities matrix;
- training curriculum overview;
- KPI dashboard outline;
- risk register with mitigation actions.

A recommended response structure is:
- Objective
- Scope and assumptions
- Key findings
- Risks and constraints
- Recommended next steps
- Open decisions requiring human input

Outputs MUST clearly identify whether each item is:
- a fact from the repository;
- an inference;
- an assumption;
- a recommendation;
- an unresolved question.

Where the repository is silent, the agent MUST say so instead of inventing a decision or authority.

## Operational assumptions and repository fit

This repository is designed as a governance and organisational-change framework rather than as a technical implementation specification. Agents should therefore:
- focus on strategy, maturity, role design, culture, governance, and continuous improvement;
- avoid turning the material into a generic product checklist or a false implementation blueprint;
- treat examples such as standards, tools, platforms, or workflows as illustrative unless explicitly configured in the repository itself;
- avoid assuming a deployment target, production environment, service account, approval workflow, or enforcement architecture exists.

If a real implementation requirement is requested, agents MUST identify it as a future design decision rather than a repository-provided fact.

## Human review and escalation

Agents SHOULD escalate to a human reviewer when a recommendation would involve:
- strategic trade-offs or budget decisions;
- organisational structure or role changes;
- legal or compliance interpretation;
- HR, hiring, compensation, or staff allocation;
- external communication, public claims, or regulatory wording.

Agents MUST stop and ask for clarification if the user requests actions that would exceed the repository's role as a strategic knowledge base or require unsupported authority.

This file should remain concise, readable, and operationally useful. It should be updated when:
- the repository's purpose evolves;
- new guidance sources are added or contradicted;
- the operational scope or audience changes;
- the repository becomes more technical, policy-oriented, or implementation-specific.
