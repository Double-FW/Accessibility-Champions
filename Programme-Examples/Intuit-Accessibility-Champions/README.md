# Intuit’s Accessibility Champions programme: an adaptable example

## Why this example is here

Intuit’s Accessibility Champions programme is presented here as a documented, evolving programme that can help other organisations think through their own design. It is not a ready-made package to copy. The choices that made sense for Intuit need to be tested against each organisation’s work, people, culture, capacity and customers.

This overview is an original Double FW summary. It does not reproduce the source article. It describes the programme’s design, the questions it raises and the limits of the available evidence.

## The design in brief

The programme described by Ted Drake at Intuit uses three levels to offer an entry point for a broad group of colleagues, then give people who want to contribute more a route towards local and wider leadership.

- **Level 1 — getting started:** a short, accessible introduction to disability etiquette, inclusive design and the organisation’s accessibility resources. Participants make a personal commitment. Additional practical activities are tailored to technical roles, such as trying an accessibility evaluation and recording an issue.
- **Level 2 — product and local leadership:** deeper learning and applied work. The article describes activities spanning inclusive content, assistive technology, product or service review, customer research, employee networks and community engagement.
- **Level 3 — wider leadership:** sustained contribution, mentoring, teaching and sharing learning, customer problem-solving, community participation and building a portfolio of work. The level is designed to recognise leadership from different roles, not only people who want a full-time accessibility career.

The levels connect learning with practice. As people progress, they can contribute to their teams, help colleagues learn, connect with customers and peers, and share useful knowledge more widely. The model combines mandatory core activities with optional or role-specific routes, rather than assuming every colleague needs the same technical training.

## Programme features worth examining

### A low-barrier way to begin

The entry level invites people to learn and participate without first being an accessibility specialist. This can broaden the community and give colleagues a shared starting point. Its value depends on accessible learning, clear expectations and support after the introduction; a badge by itself does not create capability.

### Different routes for different roles

The article distinguishes general learning from activities relevant to designers, engineers and mobile developers. It also includes non-technical ways to review work and contribute. This is a useful design prompt: a programme can share a common purpose while varying practice, tools and evidence by role or service.

### Progress through contribution

The later levels ask participants to apply learning, support other people and share what they discover. Examples include reviewing a product or service, helping colleagues use assistive technology, presenting internally, mentoring, responding to a customer problem and contributing to community work. These activities make progression about contribution and influence, not simply course completion.

### Connection beyond the employer

The programme encourages links with employee groups and external disability-related communities. External engagement can bring peer learning and reciprocal contribution into the programme. It should be planned with suitable partners and should never make disabled colleagues responsible for educating the organisation or representing every disabled person.

### Willingness to adapt

Drake describes the programme as experimental and says its requirements and opportunities changed over time. The article recounts a 2022 redesign of Level 3 after conversations with champions, managers and product teams. The redesign made progression more measurable and more applicable across roles, rather than assuming every advanced champion wanted or could take on a full-time accessibility role.

That history matters: a programme is an operating design to review, not a fixed ladder. Listen to participants and managers, examine where the activities help or create friction, and revise the model.

## What this example can and cannot establish

The article gives a detailed account of programme structure, activities and adaptation. It is useful evidence that Intuit described an operational programme with entry, development and leadership routes. It does not provide a measured evaluation demonstrating that the programme caused particular accessibility, customer or business outcomes. Treat “successful example” as a useful, sustained design example described by its creator, not as independently verified proof of impact.

A local programme should therefore define its intended outcomes and evaluate them with evidence. Participation, badges, activity counts and completed training can describe programme activity; on their own they do not demonstrate better access, conformance or user experience.

## Questions to take into your own design

- Who should be able to join, and what is a meaningful first step for people in different roles?
- Which activities build confidence, and which let people practise on real work with appropriate support?
- What progression routes exist for contributors who do not want a management or specialist career?
- Which tasks are optional, role-specific or core, and who decides?
- What time, manager support, specialist help and accessible learning materials will participants need?
- How will participants engage with disabled people and disability-related communities ethically and reciprocally?
- What evidence will show whether the programme is healthy and whether work practices or people’s experiences changed?
- How will the programme change when feedback, organisational priorities or delivery contexts change?
